Several postings accept an equivalent combination of education and experience instead of a specific degree. Every role was opened on the employer’s own board on Sunday, with pay and requirements quoted from the posting. 🔬 Deep dive: the biomanufacturing ladder

Biomanufacturing is the work of making a biological product reliably, at scale, to a standard a regulator will accept. It splits into tracks that hire differently: upstream, where cells grow; downstream, where the product is purified; analytical and QC, which measures whether it worked; MSAT, which moves a process from development into a plant and keeps it running; and the newer process-data work that ties them together. What they share is that nothing counts until it is measured and written down.

The ladder (pay from roles live this week)

The degree gate here is lower than the sector’s reputation suggests, but it is phrased differently from other fields. Rather than saying no degree is required, these postings give a ladder of equivalents: PhD and 2 years, master’s and 7, or bachelor’s and 10 at Vaxcyte; PhD and 4, or bachelor’s and 12, or an equivalent combination at Generate. Time served is treated as interchangeable with a credential, which is good news if you have the years.

One rung is worth naming on its own. Vaxcyte’s Process Engineer I role is titled as engineering, but read the duties: acquiring and evaluating manufacturing data from internal teams and contract sites, building the systems that capture protocols, study results and the reasoning behind decisions, and leading data governance across regulatory, quality, R&D and IT. That is a data and standards job, and it asks for a bachelor’s and eight years rather than a computing degree. If you have run processes and you like data, this is the door.

Pay: the middle of the ladder is wide

Associate roles start near $81,000–89,000, process development sits at $105,000–130,000, and scientist and senior scientist roles run $121,000–175,000. Bioprocess software pays more at the same level of experience: Culture Biosciences posts $130,000–165,000 for a remote control-systems engineer with four years. Two of the entry roles post no pay at all, which is worth asking about early.

Transition paths

[[IMG:transition-paths]]

If you run a process today, the quickest way to make yourself legible to these teams is to show your work with data. Take one process you know, pull the run records, and write up what changed and why: yield at each step, the variation between batches, what you would do differently. That document is your portfolio for this sector, and it is the same artifact the senior roles above are asking someone to own.

🧰 Tool and 📚 learning resource

🧰 Tool: CADET. Open-source software from Forschungszentrum Jülich for modelling chromatography, the step where most downstream yield is won or lost. It is free, documented, and a mechanistic model of a purification step you actually know is the most senior-sounding portfolio piece a downstream person can build without plant access.

📚 Learning resource: MIT OpenCourseWare 10.442, Biochemical Engineering. Free course materials from Professor Kristala Prather covering microbial growth kinetics, continuous fermentation, mass transfer and scale-up, with syllabus, readings and assignments. It dates from 2005 and covers no data science at all, which is exactly why it is still useful: the physics of scale-up has not changed.

📄 Paper of the week

Multiproperty optimization of therapeutic antibodies through sequence editing via large language model and reinforcement learning · Qiu, … Zhang · Huazhong Agricultural University, Hubei Hongshan Laboratory and Wuhan Huamei Biotech · Nature Communications, Oct 26, 2026 · open access

Improving one property of an antibody usually costs you another: bind harder and it starts sticking to things it shouldn’t. This team built a method that edits the sequence in two stages, choosing which positions to change before a language model proposes the changes, and scores variants on several properties at once. They then made 21 variants of three real antibodies and measured them. Eleven improved. This week’s video walks through the method step by step, every result with its numbers, and what the work does not show.
